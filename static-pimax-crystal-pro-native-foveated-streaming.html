Wireless VR Just Ditched the Codec — In the Pixels That Matter

Pimax's new Crystal Pro combines eye tracking with an optional 60GHz AirLink to send the GPU's native image directly to the part of the display you're looking at. If the shipping hardware matches the claim, it attacks one of high-end wireless PC VR's most stubborn compromises.

Published 7 October 2026 · STATIC

Wireless PC VR has always made a trade: lose the cable, then compress the image hard enough to fit through the wireless link. Pimax thinks it has another answer. The new Crystal Pro uses eye tracking and an optional third-generation 60G AirLink to send the GPU's native image to the region of the panels you are actually looking at, while spending less bandwidth elsewhere.

Pimax calls the system Native Foveated Streaming. The company says its 60GHz link can move up to 6 Gbps through two room antennas in a 2×2 MIMO configuration, delivering an image equivalent to 2880 × 2880 per eye at up to 90Hz in the gaze region without a video encode/decode step. Pimax claims the AirLink itself adds less than 2ms of latency.

Confirmed by Pimax: Crystal Pro was announced on 6 October 2026. The headset costs $1,399; the bundle with third-gen 60G AirLink is $1,899; AirLink alone is $599. Shipping dates have not been announced. A $1 reservation currently joins the waiting list.

Why “no codec” is the interesting part

Normal wireless VR has to encode the PC's rendered frame into video, transmit it, then decode it at the headset. Modern codecs can look excellent, but compression can still show up as smearing, banding or lost fine detail — exactly the sort of defects that become irritating when the display is centimetres from your eyes.

Pimax's idea is to stop treating every pixel equally. Integrated eye tracking samples at 120Hz. The 60G AirLink then concentrates its bandwidth on the area under your gaze, where Pimax says the GPU's own image reaches the display without video encoding. Outside that region, the system can spend less bandwidth because human peripheral vision resolves less detail.

The catch: 60GHz has rules

This is not magic Wi-Fi. Pimax says the optional AirLink uses two room antennas and requires line of sight; 60GHz does not pass through walls. The dual-antenna setup is intended to keep the connection alive if one path is briefly blocked and to split the data stream for additional bandwidth.

The headset still includes ordinary Wi-Fi streaming and a dedicated Wi-Fi adapter in the box, so the 60GHz hardware is an enthusiast upgrade rather than a requirement. DisplayPort 1.4a remains available as an optional wired PC VR path.

That distinction matters. The headline feature is not “Crystal Pro is wireless.” Plenty of headsets are. The experiment here is whether a dedicated high-bandwidth local link plus gaze tracking can make wireless PC VR look closer to a native wired feed without simply throwing an impossible amount of bandwidth at the whole frame.

The headset itself is aggressively high-end

Crystal Pro uses 2880 × 2880 QLED panels per eye — roughly 17 million pixels total — with a 110-degree horizontal field of view, 35 pixels per degree, refresh modes at 72, 90 and 120Hz, and Mini-LED local dimming with 576 zones per eye. Pimax lists an RTX 2070 as the minimum PC GPU and an RTX 3080 or RTX 4070-class GPU for the recommended experience.

There is also a Snapdragon XR2 Gen1 inside, so Crystal Pro can work as a standalone headset when a PC is not required. Power comes from an external 16,000mAh battery pack rather than a battery built into the headset.

Pimax also built the PC it wants you to use

The same Frontier event introduced Spation, a compact high-end VR PC. Pimax says the standard configuration pairs a Ryzen 7 9800X3D-class CPU with either a Radeon RX 9070 XT or GeForce RTX 5070 Ti, 32GB DDR5-6000 and a 1TB NVMe SSD. It supports Windows and SteamOS, and Pimax says a dedicated Pimax Play Linux client is coming.

What we know — and what still needs independent testing

The specifications, prices and architecture above come directly from Pimax. The important performance numbers — up to 6 Gbps, less than 2ms added AirLink latency and unencoded native imagery in the gaze region — are manufacturer claims, not STATIC lab measurements.

We still need independent testing of image quality, gaze-transition visibility, occlusion behaviour, tracking stability, battery life and end-to-end motion-to-photon latency. Pimax also has not announced a shipping date, which makes this a newly announced platform rather than a finished retail verdict.

STATIC's take: foveated rendering already asks a sensible question: why render peripheral pixels at full cost when your eye cannot resolve them? Pimax is applying the same logic to the wireless pipe. If the gaze hand-off is invisible and the latency claim holds up, that is a much more interesting solution than simply compressing harder.
