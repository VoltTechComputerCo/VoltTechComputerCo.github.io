A Brief, Enraging History of Gamers Getting Screwed by GPU Prices

2026-08-27

Somewhere out there is a parallel universe where GPU prices just... followed normal consumer electronics logic. Newer generation launches, older generation gets cheaper, everyone moves on with their lives. We do not live in that universe. We live in the one where graphics cards have spent roughly a decade taking turns being held hostage by an ever-rotating cast of reasons that have almost nothing to do with gaming.

Let's take a lap through it, mostly for the catharsis.

Round one: cryptocurrency discovers your GPU exists

The first real gut-punch. Ethereum mining turned out to run beautifully on consumer graphics cards, and for a stretch of time a mid-range GPU could pay for itself mining coins faster than it could render a game at high settings — which meant miners were, quite rationally from their side, willing to pay more for a card than the person who just wanted to play something. Shelves emptied. Scalpers appeared like a seasonal pest. A whole subculture of "is this card mining-damaged" secondhand paranoia was born and honestly never fully went away.

Round two: everyone gets sent home at once

Just as things were settling, a global pandemic arrived and did two things simultaneously that should never happen at the same time: it made an enormous number of people suddenly want a gaming PC or upgrade, right as it also broke the global supply chain that makes the components for gaming PCs. Add another crypto surge on top for good measure, and you get the single worst period in modern memory to actually buy a graphics card for the actual price on the actual box.

Round three: the AI boom, which is somehow still going

Just as crypto mining's grip loosened — thanks in part to Ethereum itself moving away from the mining model that made it all profitable in the first place — a new, considerably larger appetite showed up. AI training and inference workloads run beautifully on the same underlying hardware as gaming GPUs, and unlike a speculative crypto boom that can collapse overnight, this demand is backed by some of the best-funded companies on the planet buying at a scale individual gamers simply cannot compete with. Consumer gaming cards aren't always the direct target, but the manufacturing capacity, the high-end silicon, and the pricing psychology all bleed downward regardless.

The pattern underneath all of it

Here's the uncomfortable throughline: gamers were never really the primary customer these companies were optimising for during any of these periods — just the customer base that happened to be loudest about it and least equipped to outbid whoever actually was. Consumer graphics cards sit at an unfortunate intersection of "genuinely excellent at parallel computation" and "produced by only a couple of companies with genuinely limited fabrication capacity," which means any sufficiently large non-gaming use case for that same silicon is going to elbow gamers toward the back of the queue, pricing included, every single time.

None of this is a conspiracy against gamers specifically. It's just what happens when your hobby's core hardware turns out to be useful for something with a much bigger budget than you have.

So what do you actually do with this information

Mostly, adjust expectations. "Wait for a good price" used to be reasonable advice with a predictable rhythm to it — new generation launches, previous generation drops. It's less reliable now, because the thing that determines pricing is increasingly happening in a market that has nothing to do with games at all. The more genuinely useful habit: buy when you need to upgrade and a specific card meets your actual budget and actual needs, rather than trying to time a market that's being shaped by forces with a lot more money than you and zero interest in your frame rate.

And if it's any comfort at all: every single one of these squeezes has eventually eased. Not on a schedule anyone can predict, and not back to some mythical "fair" baseline that probably never really existed — but eased, every time, so far.
