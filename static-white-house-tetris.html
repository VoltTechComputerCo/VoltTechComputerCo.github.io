The White House Made a Border-Wall Tetris Game. Tetris Is Not Amused.

2026-09-05

The White House has launched an official “Arcade” full of retro-style games promoting Trump administration policies. One of them is a falling-block border-wall game that looks unmistakably familiar. The Tetris Company noticed — and its response was not subtle.

Reuters and the Associated Press report that the government site launched with several small browser games based on recognisable arcade mechanics. “Build the Wall” asks players to stack blocks as a border barrier, while other games borrow ideas associated with titles such as Snake and Flappy Bird.

Tetris wants its distance

The Tetris Company said it was not involved in the creation of the game and added that it takes copyright infringement seriously. It also framed its own brand around connection rather than division.

That is not the same thing as announcing a lawsuit. As of publication, we have not seen confirmation that Tetris has filed legal action. But when the owner of one of gaming’s most recognisable rule-sets publicly says “that wasn’t us” and invokes copyright, the message is pretty difficult to misread.

Can you copyright a game mechanic?

Copyright generally protects expression — artwork, code, audio and specific creative elements — rather than a broad idea like “blocks fall and you arrange them.” Game-clone disputes can still get complicated when the total presentation and behaviour get too close.

Games are now political interface design

The broader story is cultural. Governments, brands and campaigns increasingly use the visual grammar of games because people understand it instantly: progress bars, achievements, points, levels, familiar mechanics. It turns policy messaging into something shareable and interactive.

Whether that is clever communication, tasteless gamification or both depends on the audience and the subject. But once public institutions start borrowing directly from commercial game culture, the owners of that culture get a say in whether they want to be associated with it.

What happens next

The most useful thing to watch now is whether this remains a sharp social-media rebuke or turns into an actual IP dispute. Either way, “the White House made a Tetris-like border game and Tetris objected” has already earned a spot on the list of sentences we did not expect to be writing in 2026.
